# Theory: which notes, which key, which mode

## Range and tuning to the kick

Sub fundamentals that translate on a club system sit roughly between E1 (41 Hz) and
G2 (98 Hz). Reference table (scientific pitch, MIDI number, Hz; Ableton's piano roll shows
each of these one octave *lower* in name, e.g. MIDI 33 is "A0" in Live):

| Note | MIDI | Hz | Note | MIDI | Hz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | 28 | 41.2 | D2 | 38 | 73.4 |
| F1 | 29 | 43.7 | E2 | 40 | 82.4 |
| G1 | 31 | 49.0 | F2 | 41 | 87.3 |
| A1 | 33 | 55.0 | G2 | 43 | 98.0 |
| C2 | 36 | 65.4 | A2 | 45 | 110.0 |

Beatkey's key table: A minor root 55 / 5th 82.4 Hz ("full sub, clean"), C minor 65.4 / 98
("deep, cinema-grade sub"), D minor 73.4 / 110 ("warm mid-bass, pairs with Rhodes"), F minor
87.3 / 130.8 ("punchy, defined"), G minor 98 / 146.8 ("upper bass, melodic movement").

### Find the kick's pitch first
- Commercial kick fundamentals usually sit 41–55 Hz (E1–A1); tech house kicks 50–65 Hz.
- Put a Tuner (or Spectrum) on the kick and read the *sustained body*, not the transient; or
  sweep a narrow EQ bell and find the loudest resonant peak. Sample-pack labels are often a
  semitone off.
- Repitching a kick ±2 semitones is transparent; beyond that audition another sample.
- Even a couple of semitones off and "the bass feels like it's arguing with the kick".

### Three ways to coexist
1. **Same note, octave up.** Kick F1 (43 Hz) → bass centred on F2 (87 Hz). Same octave gives
   comb filtering and a phasey low end; the octave above reinforces.
2. **Consonant interval.** Fazemag's techno rule: kick in C → bass on D#, G or A (minor 3rd,
   5th, 6th). The 5th is safest.
3. **Retune or notch.** Close-but-not-identical fundamentals beat (a slow warble under every
   downbeat). Retune the kick, or notch the bass at the kick's fundamental (Myloops: "high-pass
   bass at 30 Hz, notch at kick's fundamental").

## Common keys

- House overall: A minor most common, then F, G, C, D minor. Tech house: A minor, A# minor,
  G minor (Producer School); G minor / A minor (Chris Lake).
- Deep house: A, F, G minor (modern), A / D minor (classic), D / G / C minor (Afro).
- Techno: A, D, E minor; E Phrygian.
- Sampled disco / funk is often major or Dorian: detect the sample's key before writing.

## Chord tones: the backbone

Root, 5th and octave support any chord, which is why almost every house bass pattern is built
from them. In practice:

- With chords, the bass takes the **chord root** and moves with it. MusicRadar's deep-house
  example: bass C, A, Ab; the Cm9 voicing is transposed down 3 semitones to Am9 for bar 2 and
  down 4 to Abm9 for bars 3–4. Sound On Sound notes you can build a progression the other way
  round with Live 12's Stacks: start from the bass roots and pick a chord for each by ear.
- The **5th and octave** are the passing tones of deep house ("root notes with passing tones
  on the 5th and octave").
- The **minor 7th (b7)** gives root ↔ b7 movement (10 semitones) and leads to the octave via
  the funk chromatic walk (b7 → 7 → octave, "Chameleon").
- The **b3** works as the third note of a rolling set (1 b3 5) when the stab is a minor
  triad; keep it above ~60 Hz or it muddies.
- **7ths and 9ths** belong to the chords (Am7 = A C E G is the deep house minimum; Am9 adds
  B). If an extension muddies the low end, thin the chord (Attack: delete notes, keep the 7th),
  not the bass.

## Modes and their mood

| Mode | Degrees | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Natural minor | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | Default |
| Dorian | 1 2 b3 4 5 **6** b7 | Warmer, funkier. The im7 → IV7 vamp (Am7 → D7) "is the Larry Heard sound"; Theory Helper recommends Dorian for techno basslines because the natural 6th is less dark while staying moody |
| Phrygian | 1 **b2** b3 4 5 b6 b7 | E Phrygian = C major from E. The bass "bounces around the root of E"; the flattened 2nd changes the mood completely. Flatten the 2nd of any minor key to darken a line; use the b2 as a short approach into the root or in stabs |
| Minor pentatonic / blues | 1 b3 4 5 b7 (+ b5) | Safe melodic subset; Splice suggests the blues scale for disco bass |
| Harmonic minor | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 **7** | Raised 7th as leading tone into the root (drum & bass and dark tech) |

Techno progressions that stay bass-friendly: i – iv – v – i, i – VI – III – VII,
i – i – iv – iv, i – VII – VI – VII. Deep house vamps: im7 – IVm7 (Am7 – Dm7), im7 – IV7
(Dorian), im7 – bVImaj7 – bVII7 – im7 (gospel lift, ascending bass F → G), Fm7 – Abmaj7 –
Cm7 (MAW "To Be In Love"), Am7 – Em7 – Dm7 with the 7th dropped an octave so the voicings stay
close and a descending E D C melody appears on top.

## Approach notes, passing tones, pickups

- **Chromatic approach**: a semitone below or above the target. StudyBass: "most often these
  chromatic notes fall in weak places like in between the beats, or on beats 2 & 4. Rarely on
  1 & 3." It creates "a little tension which pulls you into some other note".
- **Passing tone**: the semitone between two notes a whole step apart.
- **Pickup / walk**: the Stussy bar-4 fill is a chromatic walk from the 5th to the next tonic in
  slots 13–16 (E F F# G → A). Once per 4 or 8 bars.
- **Anticipation**: chord change on the "and" of 4 instead of beat 1.
- Splice: "occasional chromatic passing tones can give tech house a bit more grit and tension".

## Pedal tone and ostinato

A pedal holds the root while chords above it move: stable, then dissonant, then resolved, with
no extra notes. The Beatkey "Minimal Deep Loop" is one Am9 for four bars with the filter
automated over 8–16 bars. An ostinato (every rolling line) can move to the 5th or b7 only in
bar 8.

## Writing method

Fazemag: write the pattern first with a plain sound (sine, piano, GM preset) so you judge the
notes and rhythm, not the patch. "If it grooves with a simple sound it will groove more with
the fat one." The Producer School agrees: "the difference is almost never the synth".

## Tension without new pitches

Before adding notes, try: octave jump (303box: alternating low and high versions of one note
is the fastest route to the bouncing robotic character); a rest; a ghost; a longer gate on one
offbeat; moving the accent; a slide into the root; a half-step dip on beat 3 that returns on
beat 4. "Can You Feel It" is six notes, Juno-60 and TR-909, recorded in one take.
