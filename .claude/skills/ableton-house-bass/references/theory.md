# Theory: which notes, which key, which mode

## Range and tuning to the kick

Sub fundamentals that translate on a club system sit roughly between E1 (41 Hz) and
G2 (98 Hz). Reference table (scientific pitch, MIDI number, Hz; Ableton's piano roll shows
each of these one octave *lower* in name, e.g. MIDI 33 is "A0" in Live):

| Note | MIDI | Hz |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | 28 | 41.2 |
| F1 | 29 | 43.7 |
| G1 | 31 | 49.0 |
| A1 | 33 | 55.0 |
| C2 | 36 | 65.4 |
| D2 | 38 | 73.4 |
| E2 | 40 | 82.4 |
| F2 | 41 | 87.3 |
| G2 | 43 | 98.0 |
| A2 | 45 | 110.0 |

Kick fundamentals in commercial house / techno kicks are usually 41–55 Hz (E1–A1). Three
ways to coexist:

1. **Octave up.** Kick on F1 (43 Hz), bass centred on F2 (87 Hz). Same note, no comb
   filtering, the kick owns the bottom octave.
2. **Consonant interval.** A techno rule of thumb: kick in C → bass on D#, G or A (minor 3rd,
   5th, 6th). The 5th is safest; the minor 3rd and 6th add colour.
3. **Retune or notch.** If the kick rings a few cents off the bass root the two beat against
   each other (a slow warble under every downbeat). Retune the kick a semitone or two, or take a
   narrow dynamic-EQ dip out of the bass at the kick's fundamental.

Avoid the bass fundamental *below* the kick in house (the kick loses its weight); techno
"rumble" is the exception because the rumble *is* the kick, processed.

## Common keys

- House overall: A minor, F minor, G minor, C minor, D minor (A minor the most common).
- Deep house: A minor, F minor, G minor.
- Techno: A minor, D minor, E minor, E Phrygian.

These are commercial-chart observations, not rules; they are common because their roots and
5ths fall in the 41–98 Hz sweet spot.

## Chord tones: the backbone

Root, 5th and octave support any chord, major or minor, which is why almost every classic
bass pattern is built from them (root → 5th → octave up is the first pattern every bass
player learns). In house:

- The bass takes the **chord root** when there are chords; when the chord moves, the bass
  moves with it (MusicRadar's deep-house example transposes the whole Cm9 voicing down to Am9
  when the bass goes C → A).
- The **minor 7th (b7)** is the next most useful bass tone: it leads up to the octave (the funk
  b7 → 7 → octave chromatic walk, as in "Chameleon") and sits under m7 / m9 chords without
  clashing.
- The **3rd** is risky in the sub register (minor 3rds get muddy below ~60 Hz); use it an
  octave up or in a mid layer.
- The **9th and 7th** belong to the chords (deep house lives on m7 and m9 voicings). Keep
  them above the bass; if a chord extension muddies the low end, remove it from the chord,
  not the bass.

## Modes and their mood

| Mode | Degrees | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Natural minor (Aeolian) | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | Default for all four styles |
| Dorian | 1 2 b3 4 5 **6** b7 | Warmer, funkier; the natural 6th gives groovy / deep lines a lift without going major. Recommended for basslines that move |
| Phrygian | 1 **b2** b3 4 5 b6 b7 | Dark, tense; the b2 is the tech-house / techno trick (E Phrygian = C major from E). Use the b2 as a short approach into the root, not as a resting note |
| Minor pentatonic | 1 b3 4 5 b7 | Safe subset for melodic "bass as lead" lines |
| Harmonic minor | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 **7** | The raised 7th as a leading tone into the root for dramatic pickups |

Flattening the 2nd of any minor key is a one-note way to darken a line you already have.

## Approach notes, passing tones, pickups

- **Chromatic approach**: a semitone below or above the target, placed on a weak position
  (steps 2, 4, 6 … or the 16th before a beat). It creates a pull into the target.
- **Passing tone**: when two main notes are a whole step apart, the semitone between them
  connects them.
- **Pickup**: the last 16th (step 16) or last 8th (steps 15–16) of a bar leading into the next
  root. Classic funk / disco move; one pickup every 4 or 8 bars is enough.
- **Double approach**: two notes into the target (e.g. b7 → 7 → root) for the end of an
  8-bar phrase.
- **Anticipation**: play the chord change on the "and" of 4 instead of beat 1 so the line
  leans into the next bar. Pairs with tresillo accents (1, "and" of 2, "and" of 4).

Unstable notes go on weak positions; strong beats (1 and 3) take chord tones.

## Pedal tone and ostinato

A **pedal** is a bass note held or repeated while the chords above it change. It starts as
a chord tone, becomes dissonant as the harmony moves, and resolves when the harmony returns:
tension and release with zero extra notes. Deep house uses it constantly (root pedal under a
i – iv – VI – VII movement in the pads). An **ostinato** is a repeating figure that can be
melodic and rhythmic (every rolling techno bass is one). Combine them: the ostinato stays
on the root for 7 bars and moves to the 5th or b7 for bar 8.

## Tension without new pitches

Before adding notes, try: an octave jump; a rest where a note was; a ghost note; a longer
gate on one offbeat; velocity accent moved to a different step; a slide (overlapping notes)
into the root. A six-note bassline ("Can You Feel It") built a genre.
